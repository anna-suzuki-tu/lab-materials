Act 1
The greatest enemy is perfectionism
Draft Mindset — Know what the struggle really is, and separate generation from editing

Not being able to write is normal

If it flows easily, it is not new

Struggling is a marker of novelty — struggling to write is evidence that you are challenging the unknown. Uncertainty is a marker of scientific novelty. At the stage where an idea "seems sort of sayable," you have not yet reached the real question.

Paradoxically, the "ugly first draft" is the entrance to originality. Text that flows easily is a reproduction of what you already know. The struggle is not an obstacle to eliminate but a signal that "the novelty is here."

The struggle is not a matter of ability; it is evidence of challenging the unknown.

Separate generation from editing

The writing brain and the editing brain do not run at the same time

Multi-stage drafting — "Perfect Draft Syndrome," trying to write perfect sentences from the start, cuts writing speed to one tenth. The solution is to separate the writing stage and the editing stage into different time slots.

First draft (generation)
  • A dump only you will see
  • Precision does not matter, do not stop
  • Spoken language is fine
Revision (editing)
  • Done later, as a separate step
  • Elegance is added here
  • Reread with a reviewer's eyes
Put "writing time" and "editing time" in your calendar as separate slots.

Prescriptions for when you are stuck

Not willpower, but an intervention matched to the symptom

Tactical fixes for writer's block — there are three kinds of symptoms of being stuck, and a different intervention works for each.

1
Fear of the blank page
Ten minutes of freewriting — dump it out regardless of precision. You may throw away what you wrote
2
Loss of momentum
The WIRMI method — rewrite in spoken language: "What I really mean is…". Saying it aloud loosens the block
3
Feeling overwhelmed
Break the task down to "write the caption of Figure 1." Decide only the next step, not the finish
Suzuki-A. Lab's Writing Paper Skills turn these prescriptions into a system.

Workflow: The first draft is an "unfolding task"

Once Framing and Figures are done, the Draft becomes an unfolding task

From weekly habit to submission — you do not need to start writing from a blank page. If the materials are in place, the first draft is an unfolding task.

1
Framing / Figures
Fix the structure of the figures and the framework of the paper first (see Lab OS 03 and 04; details in Writing Papers — Figures)
2
Paper card (updated weekly)
The weekly habit of keeping the one-sentence claim "Compared to X, we Y" updated
3
The one-sentence claim is ready
If you have a claim you can state outright, the skeleton of the first draft is done
4
Draft = unfolding
Just unfold the assembled materials into text. Write from materials, not from a blank page (details in Writing Papers — Draft)
Here is this week's habit, so that you do not panic in December.

Writing Paper Skills — The "form" that makes claims stronger

Suzuki-A. Lab has put into words the "form" that makes claims stronger

The strength of a paper is decided by the strength of its claim. A manuscript that ends up writing "we observed X" and one that states outright "we enabled X for the first time" get completely different reactions from reviewers. Writing Paper Skills is this "form for stating outright," made explicit as a process to run together with AI.

BEFORE
  • Ends up writing "we observed X"
  • The claim is weak; the novelty does not come through
AFTER
  • States outright "we enabled X for the first time"
  • Reviewers' objections are answered in advance
The process run together with AI is Writing Paper Skills (contents in the next section).

Writing Paper Skills — Five checks

Check sentence by sentence, through the eyes of 3–4 imagined reviewers

Five checks, sentence by sentence — check each sentence against the following five items.

①
Claim-strength
Is the level of claim strength appropriate — are observed / indicates / suggests used distinctly?
②
Reviewer objection
Where will the reviewer push — is the anticipated objection pre-empted?
③
Evidence alignment
Are evidence and claim in balance — is the claim as strong as the data can support?
④
Comparison validity
Is the comparison valid — same physical quantity, same conditions?
⑤
Safer rewrite
Is it a safe rewrite without an AI smell — check dashes, stock phrases, and quantified exaggerations
There are two modes of use.

Writing Paper Skills — Two ways to use it

Draft-diagnosis mode and supervisor mode are different things

Two modes, one purpose: sharpen the claim — both modes share the same core principle, "make the claim stronger."

Student mode: Draft diagnosis
  • Check what is being said vs. what should be said
  • Take the "form" home through before / after
  • No clean copy — the next one you do yourself
Supervisor mode: Working draft
  • Working draft → expressing what feels off
  • Translate what feels off into a claim
  • Final responsibility for the text stays with the author
Common principle: AI is a drafting device, not the writer. Being able to say, sentence by sentence, "why it is there" is the author's final responsibility.
AI is a tool. Only humans can bear responsibility.
Act 2
A paper is not complete with the text alone
Beyond the manuscript — Authors, acknowledgements, data, reuse, AI

Authorship — The four criteria

Authorship requires all four criteria

Under the ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) criteria, an author must meet all four of the following. If even one is missing, the person is not an author.

①
Substantial contribution
Substantial contribution to conception, data acquisition, analysis, or interpretation
②
Drafting or revising
Drafting the manuscript, or critically revising it for important intellectual content
③
Final approval
Final approval of the very version to be published
④
Accountability
Accountability for all aspects of the work — all four together make an author
"Data collection only" or "funding only" is not authorship; it goes in the acknowledgements.

Authorship — When to talk about it

Discuss author order and responsibilities before you start writing

Talk early, not at submission — the most draining authorship troubles are disputes right before submission. Agree on author order, first author, corresponding author, and the roles of co-authors during the project's Framing period.

If in doubt, consult Suzuki. The final decision is made in consultation with your supervisor. The same goes for acknowledgements: always obtain the person's consent before listing them.
The time to talk is the Framing period — right before submission is too late.

Data Repository — Data is part of the paper

An era in which data is reviewed as part of the paper

Open science is the default — many journals, including Geothermics, have data-sharing policies. Follow the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) and publish in a repository with a DOI (Zenodo, etc.).

Required README items: file naming conventions, variable definitions, software requirements, ORCID. Dates in ISO 8601 (YYYY-MM-DD). State the license explicitly, CC0 or CC-BY. Grant numbers must be exact (LOTUS etc. — do not forget to check the funding conditions).
Data is a package for an unknown someone in the future. Hand it over with a README and a license.

Self-plagiarism

Even your own text becomes self-plagiarism when copy-pasted

The tolerance for text recycling differs by section. Take particular care when turning a master's thesis into a journal paper.

SectionReuseConditions / cautions
Methods○Generally accepted if the source is cited
Introduction△Wholesale copying is not allowed. Substantial rewriting is required
Results / Discussion✕Reuse amounts to duplicate publication. Always write anew
Master's thesis → journal paper is the most dangerous moment.

AI use — Why AI cannot be an author, and the rules of Suzuki-A. Lab

AI cannot be an author — because it cannot bear responsibility

Deficit of accountability & confidentiality — AI cannot sign a copyright agreement and cannot bear ethical responsibility.

Risks
  • All responsibility for hallucinations (fake citations) lies with the author
  • Uploading a paper under review is a breach of confidentiality and copyright
  • Violation of publisher policies (Springer Nature: generative-AI figures prohibited in principle, etc.)
Suzuki-A. Lab rules
  • If used, disclose tool name, version, and purpose in Methods / acknowledgements
  • Never enter unpublished manuscripts — absolutely
  • Keep a usage record and check the disclosure statement before submission
Only humans can bear responsibility — hence records and disclosure.
Act 3
The first thing the editor reads is not the paper
The cover letter — Most desk rejections are decided here

The role of the cover letter

The cover letter is a 90-second pitch to the editor

Your first (and maybe only) pitch — most desk rejections (rejection without review) are decided at the stage of reading the cover letter. A copy-paste of the abstract is spotted instantly.

You are expected to write three points — "why this journal, why now, what is new" — in your own words. Editors are busy researchers handling dozens of submissions every day. If these three points do not come through clearly, the journey ends before it reaches the reviewers.

A copy-paste of the abstract is spotted instantly. Tell the three points in your own words.

Four axes of persuasion

Speak along four axes: trust, logic, curiosity, timing

Ethos, Logos, Pathos, Kairos — the four axes of persuasion organized by ancient Greek rhetoric apply directly to the cover letter.

Ethos (trust)
Declaration of ethical compliance and no duplicate submission. Show that the authors understand this journal's rules
Logos (logic)
The difference from prior work, in plain words. Make "what is different" clear without leaning on jargon
Pathos (curiosity)
Hint at an elegant solution to a long-standing problem. Pose a question the reader wants to read about
Kairos (timing)
Why now, and why this journal. Show the timely reason and the fit with the journal
If the four axes cannot be filled, the story of the submission is not yet settled.

Act 4
Peer review is not criticism; it is free consulting
The review process — From submission to publication, the conversation begins

The whole process

From submission to publication, a conversation of several months on average

What actually happens after you click submit

1
Submission
The editor acknowledges receipt and checks scope and format. If it does not fit: desk rejection
2
Review (1–3 months)
2–4 reviewers evaluate anonymously. The editor invites and chases the reviewers
3
The decision arrives
Four kinds: Accept / Minor revision / Major revision / Reject. Major is a sign of "still alive"
4
Revision → re-review
Submit the response letter and the revised manuscript. The re-review → decision loop may continue once or twice
Major revision is a sign of "still alive." It is not a Reject. It is evidence that the editor judged "this can be published if fixed." Respond carefully and there is a good chance of acceptance.
When the decision arrives — first, manage your emotions.

Managing emotions

For harsh comments, a 72-hour cooling-off period

Never reply in anger — the emotional reaction on first reading the review comments never goes into the response letter.

1
Do not write a rebuttal on first reading
Wait 24–72 hours before designing the response. Vent the anger in a memo; do not put it in the response letter
2
A reviewer's misunderstanding = your insufficient explanation
Start with "As you point out, our explanation was insufficient." Do not blame the reviewer
3
Separate emotion from response
Feeling angry is normal. But carrying that emotion into the response letter lowers the acceptance rate
Once you have cooled down — on to the craft of the response letter.

The craft of the response letter

Make the response letter self-contained, and write the conclusion first

Self-contained, answer-first responses — a good response letter can be understood without the reviewer opening the revised manuscript.

A bad response letter
  • Starts with a long rebuttal
  • Makes the reviewer open the manuscript and search
  • The conclusion comes last
A good response letter
  • States Yes / No / Revised explicitly at the top
  • Quotes the revised text directly in the response letter
  • Distinguishes comment, response, and revised text by color and indentation
Reducing the reviewer's effort is the shortest path to acceptance.

Dealing with an unfair review

The opponent is not the reviewer; fight through a letter to the editor

If an unfair attack by a competitor is suspected, do not fight directly in the response letter.

Response letter (public)
  • Stick to calm, fact-based responses
  • Do not confront the reviewer directly
  • Show your handling of the comments carefully
Letter to the editor (confidential)
  • Convey the concern about an unfair attack by a competitor in a private letter
  • Lay out facts, not emotions
  • Ask for the editor's judgment
Let us also uphold the culture of reviewing the Work, not the Peer. The peer-review process exists to evaluate the research, not the person.
There are ways to counter an unfair review. But calmly, and fact-based.
